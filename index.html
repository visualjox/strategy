Outside the Box:
How I'd Own Hip-Hop
Culture if I Ran Gully Gang

How Gully Gang could engineer a cultural moment instead of selling merch.

Vishal Joshi Brand Strategy 7 min read
Album art · 1×1

There's a kid in Dharavi who knows every word of Mere Gully Mein.

He doesn't own a Divine t-shirt. And he never will.

Not because he can't afford it. Because a ₹500 t-shirt doesn't feel like Divine. And somewhere, deep down, even Divine knows this.

That's the insight. And that's where this entire strategy is built from.

The Problem Nobody's
Talking About

Divine wears Amiri. He wears Gucci. He built a brand on scarcity, struggle, and street credibility.

And then Gully Gang sells a ₹1000 graphic tee.

That's not a merch strategy. That's brand dilution dressed up as a revenue stream. Every cheap drop chips away at the mythology Divine spent years building. Most creative directors would double down on it. Add more SKUs. Do a festival collab. Maybe a concert film.

We're not doing any of that.

The Play

We're not launching merch. We're engineering a cultural moment.

It starts with one decision that most creative directors would never make:

We don't go to Nike. We don't go to Adidas. We go to Gully Labs.

And if you haven't heard of Gully Labs yet, that's exactly the point.

Gully Labs is one of India's most quietly exciting independent sneaker labels. No celebrity endorsements. No mall presence. No algorithmic marketing. Just clean design, deliberate drops, and a community that treats their sneakers less like footwear and more like cultural artifacts.

They have a shoe lineup called Baaz.

Read that again.

Gully Labs — 1×1

Baaz. Divine's most iconic record. Baazigar. The mythology he built his entire identity around.

Baaz — 1×1
Baaz — 1×1

This isn't a collab. This is a convergence. Two brands that were built from the streets, for the streets, whose names were practically destined to exist in the same sentence. Gully Gang x Gully Labs isn't a partnership you pitch in a boardroom. It's a story that writes itself.

Here's how we build it:

  • —No announcement. The collab is never officially confirmed until the shoe drops. Every piece of communication before that is designed to make the audience feel like they're discovering something, not being sold something.
  • —The name stays Baaz. We don't rename it. We don't co-brand it aggressively. The shoe lives within Gully Labs' existing lineup but carries Divine's DNA in every design detail — the materials, the colorway, the craftsmanship rooted in his story.
  • —The design language is deliberate. Every element connects to a chapter of Nange Pair — the documentary we're building in parallel. The sole. The lace color. The insole print. Each one a reference that only makes sense once you've watched the series.
  • —The collab is positioned as convergence, not commerce. Two things that were always meant to exist together finally did. Not "Divine drops a shoe." But "Baaz found its voice."

And honestly? That positioning alone does more work than any campaign budget could.

Before the Shoe,
the Man

Before anyone sees the shoe though, they see the man.

We launch a documentary series. Nange Pair.

Not the recycled rags-to-riches narrative that every hip-hop documentary defaults to. A deeper cut. The untold threads. The tension between the streets that made him and the stages that claimed him. Each episode structured around a theme that connects directly to the Baaz shoe — without ever showing it.

  • —Episode 1: The streets he ran on barefoot
  • —Episode 2: The first time he wore something that felt like him
  • —Episode 3: What it means to build something that lasts
Nange Pair — Documentary thumbnail · 16×9

Nange Pair — the series that drops one month before the tour.

Each episode ends with a loose thread that pulls toward something. The audience knows something is coming. They just don't know what. And that feeling? That's the whole game.

The series drops one month before the tour. The tour is called Nange Pair.

And at every concert on this tour, Divine performs barefoot. No press release. No explanation. Just the image — and the internet does the rest. The documentary starts trending. The Baaz collab becomes the most anticipated drop in Indian sneaker history before a single product photo exists.

We feed the algorithm just enough:

  • —A blurry first look posted from a studio session
  • —A Gully Labs employee spotted wearing something unfamiliar
  • —A single frame in the Nange Pair trailer where the shoe is half in frame for 0.3 seconds

Controlled chaos. Maximum surface tension.

Then we release 222 pairs. One date. No ads. No influencers. No restock.

The resale market becomes the PR campaign.

Why This Works

This is what separates brand architecture from brand management.

Most labels and creative teams optimise for short-term liquidity — merch drops, festival revenue, streaming numbers. What they miss is the compounding value of mythologised scarcity. Travis Scott didn't become a billionaire brand because he made good music. He became one because every touchpoint — the tour, the documentary, the shoe, the chaos at the drop — fed a singular, consistent narrative.

Divine already has the raw material. The street credibility. The cultural resonance. The audience that treats his story like scripture.

This strategy doesn't manufacture hype. It organises what already exists into a sequence that builds irreversible cultural equity.

222 pairs. No ads. No dilution.
Just a mythology, engineered deliberately.
